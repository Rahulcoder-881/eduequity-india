Education for Unprivileged Communities in India: Analytical Report
Education | UNICEF India
Image: Children (including a child in a wheelchair) in an Indian school classroom (source: UNICEF India). India has greatly expanded schooling, but millions of “unprivileged” children – from poor, rural, tribal, minority or disabled backgrounds – still lack full access or learning. Key laws (RTE Act 2009) and policies (NEP 2020) guarantee free schooling for ages 6–14
, yet barriers remain. UNICEF reports some 6.1 million Indian children were out of school in 2014
, with 29% dropping out before completing elementary education – mostly from the most marginalized families
. Roughly half of adolescents never finish secondary school
. This report examines scope (who is unprivileged), barriers (economic, social, cultural, geographic, infrastructure, policy), evidence-based interventions (ECE through adult, digital) with India data, delivery models in India, costs and funding (per-student ranges, CSR, budgets, social bonds), M&E (UDISE+, ASER, etc.), scalability, policy recommendations, and a 3-year implementation roadmap. We include tables of 8–10 Indian case studies (location, target, model, cost, outcomes, funders, scalability) and a prioritized list of interventions for low-resource settings, plus a 3-year pilot budget template.

Definition & Scope
In India, unprivileged generally refers to groups excluded by poverty, geography, caste/tribe, religion, gender or disability. This includes children from rural/remote villages, urban slums, Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST), Other Backward Classes (OBC), minority religions (e.g. Muslims), girls (especially in conservative communities), persons with disabilities, and children in conflict-prone or marginalized areas (e.g. left-wing extremism zones). Government data and studies highlight these groups have lower enrolment and completion rates. The UDISE+ data show universal primary enrolment (GER ~103%) but rising dropouts by higher grades
, suggesting the remaining out-of-school population is concentrated among the hardest-to-reach. UNICEF notes that dropouts and out-of-school children in India are disproportionately from the “most marginalised” segments
. For example, girls who marry early or tribal children without mother-tongue instruction are likelier to leave school. This report assumes a nationwide view but notes that the mix of unprivileged groups varies by state: some states have more tribal children (e.g., Jharkhand), others more slum dwellers (e.g., Maharashtra), etc.

Key Barriers
Unprivileged Indian children face multiple overlapping barriers:

Economic: Poverty forces child labour or prevents buying uniforms/books. Many families can’t afford even “free” education costs. Studies show low-income households and landless families have far higher dropout rates
. Anganwadi (pre-school) enrolment is lower among poor families (cited below). Government spending on education (~3–4% GDP) is still low
, and state budgets may favor urban schools. Families often prioritize older boys’ education over girls when resources are tight.

Social & Cultural: Gender norms hinder girls: early marriage, safety/security concerns, long distances to school, lack of separate toilets or menstrual hygiene resources. UNICEF notes that girls drop out at higher rates, and many out-of-school children are girls
. Caste/Tribe: SC/ST and other minorities often attend poorer-quality schools and face discrimination. Studies find high attrition among low-caste and Muslim students
. Language: Children from tribal communities may not speak the language of instruction; UNESCO highlights 40% of Indian children don’t learn in their mother tongue, impeding learning
.

Geographic: Rural and Remote Areas: Thousands of villages lack a proximate school, forcing children to travel far or leave school. Hilly and conflict-affected regions (e.g. parts of Kashmir, Left-wing extremist areas) see frequent school closures. Seasonal migration (e.g. for work) disrupts education.

Infrastructure & Resources: Many government schools lack basic facilities. While UDISE+ reports 96.7% schools have drinking water
, only ~54% have internet, and ~89% have trained teachers
. Rural schools often have few toilets, no electricity or libraries. Teacher shortages or absenteeism in remote areas further limit education. Lower-level officials report that government school quality in marginalized regions is below par, pushing some families to hire tutors or private unregistered “kutchi pathshala” (informal schools).

Policy/Governance: While the Right to Education Act (RTE) legally guarantees free education up to age 14
, its implementation is incomplete. Some states still charge indirect fees; enforcement of norms (like pupil-teacher ratios, infrastructure standards) is weak. Inclusion policies (for SC/ST, disabled, minorities) exist but often lack funding or monitoring. Inadequate data collection on marginalized groups historically hampered targeting (UDISE+ is improving this).

Overall, India’s barriers mirror global patterns (poverty, gender, location) but are intensified by caste and language divisions. As one UNICEF report notes, dropout rates remain high (“often [among] the most marginalised children”)
. Tackling these requires multi-faceted approaches.

Evidence-Based Interventions (India-Specific)
Research and field evaluations in India identify effective interventions at all levels:

Early Childhood (0–6): The Anganwadi/ECCE system (ICDS scheme) delivers pre-school nutrition/education. Evaluations show villages with high Anganwadi quality have better school readiness
. Programs providing early learning materials to Anganwadis (e.g. state-specific models like NIPUN in Andhra Pradesh) have raised enrolment in ECCE. Home-visiting programs for parents also show promise in some pilot studies (e.g. by Gram Vikas and UNICEF).

Primary Education (6–14): Key proven interventions include: Remedial Literacy/Numeracy (TaRL): Pratham’s Teaching at the Right Level methodology, where children are re-grouped by skill level and taught via play and basic activities, has dramatically improved learning. J-PAL impact evaluations found that short TaRL learning camps doubled the number of grade‑3 children reading at grade‑level
. The Government has since scaled TaRL in several states. School Support (mid-day meals, uniforms, textbooks): The National Mid-Day Meal Scheme (covering ~120 million children) is known to boost attendance and nutrition; studies in India show MDMS increased enrollment and reduced absenteeism (especially among girls and the poor). The Right to Education’s free uniforms and books provisions likewise reduce dropout. Community Engagement: Projects that involve parents and communities (like School Management Committees under RTE) help retention. For example, in some states, mobilizing community volunteers to track out-of-school children has cut dropout.

Secondary Education (14–18): Interventions focus on reducing gender and socio-economic dropouts. Examples include cash stipends and scholarships: Programs like the Bihar ST Girls Scholarship or the Telangana Pelli Kanuka (bridal gift) grant conditional on schooling have raised female enrolment. Life Skills and Career Guidance: NGO models like Akanksha Foundation include mentorship, psychosocial support and parent engagement (see case study below). Technical and vocational integration (e.g. ITI courses, skill vouchers) help at-risk teens stay engaged.

Vocational & Skill Training: Schemes like PMKVY and Skills India provide free vocational training to rural youth; sectoral intervention (e.g. IT literacy in village clusters) has been piloted by NGOs (e.g. Digital Empowerment Foundation in Bihar). Multidimensional programs (combining skill plus life skills) by organizations like Kaivalya Education Foundation have shown increased employability.

Adult Literacy: The Saakshar Bharat Mission (up to 2022) ran 7.7 million adult literacy classes, reaching tens of millions of women. Evaluations found modest improvements in adult reading/writing rates, particularly among marginalized women in states like Rajasthan and MP. Continuing these with multi-generational learning centers (where adult women learn alongside children) has been recommended by education surveys.

Digital/Media Learning: Remote education has expanded via government and NGO initiatives: The DIKSHA platform (national e-content) and Swayam Prabha TV channels deliver lessons nationwide. During COVID-19, states like Kerala used community TV channels; UNICEF’s Learning Passport digital tool was deployed in Jharkhand’s migrant communities. Many villages now have smartphone-based tuition programs (e.g. eVidyaloka’s volunteer-teacher app connecting rural children to online tutors). UNESCO notes India’s high mobile penetration (80% rural have phones) as an opportunity. Learning radio programs (like All India Radio’s classes) have reached tribal areas. Evidence suggests blended models (part in-person, part tech) can maintain learning continuity in low-resource settings.

These interventions must be evidence-based. For instance, the NSSO and ASER surveys provide annual data on literacy/numeracy (see M&E below). Programs like Pratham’s TaRL have rigorous evaluations
. Generally, combining academic support (remedial teaching) with socio-economic support (meals, subsidies) is most effective. UNICEF’s strategy emphasizes foundational learning and life skills for the “most disadvantaged”
, aligning with proven models.

Delivery Models in India
Several models deliver education to marginalized children:

Government-Run Schemes: The backbone is formal schools under Samagra Shiksha (integrated scheme for 6–18 yrs). Inclusive education units (IEUs) and resource rooms aim to support disabled children. Some states have “open schooling” units under NIOS for dropout re-entry.
Community Schools / Alternative Schools: Inspired by global models, some Indian NGOs run community schools in slums or remote villages. For example, Pratham’s Learning Camps (TaRL) act like temporary ‘catch-up schools’ during vacations or after school. Bridge International Academies (a private chain) piloted low-cost private schools in some cities (controversial, modest reach). Mobile Schools: Nomadic or flood-prone communities use moving schools; e.g., many pupils in Ladakh’s nomadic areas are taught in yurts/geshes, and after the 2014 Kashmir floods, boat schools resumed classes on Dal Lake.
NGO–Government Partnerships: As with Akanksha Foundation, NGOs collaborate with municipal governments to run or reform existing schools
. In Rajasthan, Educate Girls partnered with the government and UNICEF, leveraging village volunteers to enrol girls and teach them basic skills
. Similarly, the Nirmal Bharat Sewa model engages local NGOs to manage school infrastructure projects, financed by block grants.
Public–Private Initiatives: CSR-driven programs (e.g. Tata’s Tata Trust scholarships, RP-Sanjiv Goenka’s STEM schools) add resources to public schools. Corporate partnerships have funded e-classrooms and smart classes in rural government schools. Results-based financing: the Educate Girls Development Impact Bond (see case study) is a pioneering example where private investors funded NGO work in government schools
.
Radio/TV & ICT: The Government’s Swayam Prabha offers 12 DTH channels with curriculum content. Community radio (e.g. in tribal Chhattisgarh) broadcasts lessons and parent messages. NGO projects like eVidyaloka connect volunteer tutors online to rural classrooms using satellite connectivity. These media models supplement limited teacher availability, especially in remote hamlets.
Blended/Remedial Models: Many non-formal education (NFE) centers combine home-study plus periodic in-person classes. For example, BRAC India (Bihar) had NFE centers where children aged 6–14 learned basic literacy; after a year 90% transitioned to regular schools. The government’s Siksha Kendras (community learning centers) perform a similar role, though on a smaller scale.
India’s delivery models must navigate complex terrain – caste barriers, remote terrain, etc. A common thread is flexibility: e.g. summer camps (TaRL), multiple shifts (as with split-shift schooling in slums), and local language teaching. The greatest successes come from models embedded in communities, such as community volunteers teaching or mobilizing enrollment
.

Cost Estimates and Funding Mechanisms
Public Expenditure: India’s education spending has risen slowly; total public education spend is ~10% of government spending (around 3.5% of GDP)
. Per-student spending in India is low compared to global averages. For example, UNESCO (2024) notes low-income countries spend ~$55 per student
; India, as a lower-middle-income country, is somewhat higher but still constrained (roughly ₹10,000–15,000 per primary pupil per year in public schools by some estimates). The Right to Education has mandated centrally sponsored schemes (e.g. Samagra Shiksha) with matching state budgets.

Donor/CSR: A significant portion of supplementary funding comes from the World Bank (Bihar Education Project, etc.), UN agencies and global funds (though India now receives less bilateral aid). The Educate Girls Challenge was funded by DFID/UKAid
. Many corporations use CSR for education; for instance, Aditya Birla and Infosys Foundations fund school construction, scholarships and teacher training. According to NITI Aayog, CSR spending on education was ₹2,700 crore ($330M) in FY2021.

Innovative Mechanisms: Social impact bonds (SIBs) have been piloted: Educate Girls’ DIB (3-year, ₹1.85 crores ≈ $250k) paid out by outcomes
. India’s first Education SIB, the USD 730k DIB in Rajasthan saw investors earn 8.9x return for meeting targets. Student/stakeholder financing: Some states have Pension/Pension schemes, where conditional cash transfers (like Bihar’s Kanya Suraksha) function like incentives for schooling. Microfinance-linked loans for education and school vouchers are rare in rural India.

Per-Student Cost Ranges: Based on government budgets and pilot programs:

In government primary schools, the unit cost (per student per year) is roughly ₹12,000–₹15,000 (including teacher salaries, materials, mid-day meals).
Accelerated learning camps (TaRL) cost roughly ₹600–800 per student per year (since they use volunteer instructors for ~50 days)
.
NGO-run community school may spend more (e.g. the Akanksha model, with professional staff, likely costs several lakhs per class of ~30 kids annually, given an estimated ~₹1,50,000 per school per year).
Adult literacy classes (~20–30 adults) often run on ₹1,000 per learner per year under Saakshar Bharat.
Funding Models: Most funding is via

Central and state budgets (e.g. Samagra Shiksha’s approx. ₹31,000 crore in FY2023).
Donor aid (e.g. World Bank projects like Saakshar Bharat, District Primary Education).
Community contributions (in Anganwadis or CLCs, parents may provide space/materials).
Stakeholder mobilization: In programs like Educate Girls, villager volunteers contributed time and some materials.
Innovative finance: SIBs as above.
In practice, scaling education for the unprivileged requires blending multiple sources: government funds for baseline schooling, supplemented by targeted aid for hard-to-reach (e.g. scholarships, mid-day meals, nonprofits).

Monitoring & Evaluation
India has robust data systems:

UDISE+ (EMIS): Comprehensive school census data (annually) now covers all government and private schools. It provides disaggregated data on enrolment by gender, caste (SC/ST), disability, infrastructure (e.g. % schools with disability ramps, toilets, power). Policymakers use UDISE+ to track enrolment gaps. For example, UDISE+ 2024-25 shows improvement in facilities (drinking water in ~97% schools) but highlights shortfalls in digital access (internet in ~54% schools)
. Analyzing UDISE+ data by district can flag areas with out-of-school children.

Learning Assessments: The Annual Status of Education Report (ASER, by Pratham) is the de facto learning audit for rural India. ASER’s 2022 report found less than half of Grade 5 students read at Grade 2 level
, demonstrating the learning crisis. Government assessments (NAS by NCERT) also test a sample of students every 3–4 years in Grades 3,5,8; the latest NAS 2017 showed ~50% of grade-3 students did not master basic competencies. State-level assessments (e.g., Tamil Nadu SSLC exam pass rates) gauge completion outcomes. Programs like TaRL embed their own assessments (as in Pratham camps
).

Out-of-School Tracking: Periodic Out-of-School Children (OOSC) surveys (by UIS, UNESCO) provide estimates (in 2019 India had ~1.4 million OOSC
). The Government’s UDISE reports also indirectly track inclusion. Some NGOs conduct local mapping of out-of-school clusters (e.g. UNICEF/UNESCO did in select states).

Program M&E: Well-run interventions have built-in metrics. TaRL tracks child progress weekly. Educate Girls DIB had third-party evaluations for targets. Schemes like Saakshar Bharat monitored enrolment in literacy classes and post-test literacy levels. Capacity-building in M&E is part of Samagra Shiksha (e.g. training Block Resource Coordinators on data). Tools like GIS mapping of schools and mobile apps for attendance (e.g. app for midday meal) are increasingly used.

Key metrics include enrolment rates (GER/NER by subgroup), retention/dropout rates (transition rates), and learning outcome benchmarks (literacy, numeracy proficiency). To evaluate quality, India is piloting the National Learning Assessment and plans a National Achievement Survey 3.0. For systemic review, NITI Aayog and independent bodies periodically audit implementation (e.g. mid-day meal implementation reports).

Scalability & Sustainability
Scaling successful models requires:

Institutionalization: Programs embedded in government systems tend to scale. TaRL was adopted into government schools in multiple states (with state curriculum integration)
. Akanksha built a “school-in-school” model within municipal schools
. Community-based programs (e.g. NGO-run evening schools) are less scalable if they rely on donations; integrating them into official schemes (like converting NFE centers to formal schools) enhances sustainability.

Funding Continuity: Government commitment of multi-year budgets is vital. Central schemes provide line budgets (e.g. INR 31,000 cr for Samagra Shiksha 2023). Sustainability also depends on cost-efficiency (low-cost per-child interventions are more replicable). The Educate Girls DIB showed that result-based financing can attract new money, but ended in 2018. Replicating such models requires identifying outcome payers (like CSR). Ongoing CSR involvement (e.g. AT&T’s commitment to literacy) can underwrite expansion.

Community Ownership: Programs succeed when communities co-own them. For example, Army Welfare Education Society’s adoption of villages for schools shows that when local people (even soldiers’ families) are involved, dropout falls. Conversely, programs that don’t engage parents (trustees or PTAs) often see slippage. Teacher training/retention is also key: models that recruit local or female teachers (e.g. Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas in tribal areas) are more stable.

Contextual Adaptation: A one-size approach seldom works. Pratham’s TaRL had to adapt languages and materials for each state. Tribal education requires mother-tongue pedagogy
. Programs must fit local calendars (monsoon can close schools, so learning camps were scheduled otherwise in some states). The NEP 2020’s emphasis on multi-stage structure (5+3+3+4) will require realigning scaled programs to the new grades.

Political and Civil Support: Political will (ministerial backing, parliament support) ensures scaling. The RTE Act’s constitutional mandate pushed states to open schools. Civil society advocacy (e.g. Right to Education Forum) pressures budgets. Conversely, political instability or frequent policy changes (state elections) can disrupt scaling.

In summary, the most scalable solutions in India have been those integrated into national/state programs with guaranteed funding (e.g., midday meals, mid-day meal education, free textbooks) or those proven at small scale that then get adopted (TaRL, chapter). Ensuring sustainability means building local capacity (training educators and parents) and blending resources (state + national + NGO/CSR).

Policy Recommendations
Based on the above, India should adopt the following policies to reach unprivileged learners:

Strengthen Free and Inclusive Education: Ensure the RTE Act’s provisions (no fees, 25% reservation in private schools for disadvantaged, infrastructure norms) are fully implemented. Expand free transport for students in hilly or remote regions. Consider extending free schooling beyond 14 (e.g. for disadvantaged girls) as a transitional measure (though NEP 2020 does this via scholarships).

Targeted Scholarships & Incentives: Scale up stipends for SC/ST and minority girls at each transition point (class 5, 8, 10) to reduce dropouts. Expand mid-day meal to include nutritious snacks for adolescents (weekly eggs/milk) to keep them in secondary school. Use cash/in-kind transfers to offset child labor opportunity costs in poor families.

Early Education & Multilingualism: Increase Anganwadi coverage in underserved areas and integrate ECCE into school blocks. Critically, implement mother-tongue instruction in pre-primary and early primary grades: UNESCO’s “Bhasha Matters” report (2025) emphasizes that learning in one’s own language improves retention
. At least for 3–5 years, teach in local language or dialect, with later gradual bilingual transition.

Quality & Learning-Focused Reforms: Mandate remedial learning periods in schools (as some states already do via TaRL) to address learning gaps early. Invest heavily in teacher training for inclusive pedagogy (handling multi-grade classes, special needs) and digital literacy. Introduce learning outcome targets into school accountability (e.g. state boards tracking foundational skills).

Leverage Technology Judiciously: Continue expanding national digital platforms (DIKSHA, Swayam Prabha) but ensure access: accelerate BharatNet to connect all villages, distribute low-cost tablets to resource centers, and support community radio in off-grid areas. Use mobile SMS/call reminders for attendance (as in some Bihar districts).

Data and M&E Emphasis: Improve EMIS by requiring all private schools to report regularly (many small private “informal” schools currently under-report). Institutionalize regular statewide learning assessments (NAS) and use findings to guide interventions. Publicize disaggregated data on SC/ST, OBC, PWD enrolments to target funds.

Public–Private and Community Partnerships: Encourage state governments to partner with NGOs under a regulated framework. For example, allow credible NGOs to run supplemental programs in government schools (Akanksha model
). Expand successful pilots (like Educate Girls) through government schemes (as has begun in Rajasthan).

Legislation and Rights Awareness: Strengthen awareness campaigns about the Right to Education, especially among marginalized communities (for example, through local panchayat meetings). Enforce child protection laws to combat practices like child marriage or bonded labor that derail schooling.

Recommended Interventions (Prioritized for Low-Resource Settings)
Anganwadi & ECCE Expansion: Leverage ICDS to reach unserved children. Ensure every village has a quality pre-school centre, with trained Anganwadi Workers and learning materials. Promote home-visits for early stimulation in poorest households.
Complete Fee Waivers and Scholarships: Abolish all formal and informal fees for SC/ST/OBC families. Institute cash transfers or scholarships (like KGBV expansion) specifically for girls, disabled children, and minority youths at each education level.
Community Learning Camps (TaRL): Implement remedial learning camps in every block using TaRL, with local volunteers. These fast-track camps should be an official part of school calendars (as in several states) to boost foundational skills.
Strengthening Mid-Day Meals & Health: Expand nutrition beyond MDM to mid-school snacks and health check-ups. Link with health workers to address anemia and vision issues which hinder learning.
School Infrastructure Upgrades: Prioritize toilets (separate for girls), ramps and drinking water in marginalized areas. Set up local child-friendly committees to report and fix infrastructure lapses.
Mother-Tongue Instruction: Start classes in local languages for at least the first 3 grades in tribal and minority-dominant areas. Invest in local-language textbooks. UNESCO reports that 40% of Indian children don't learn in their own language, so multilingual education is crucial.
Girls’ Safety and Awareness: Build “safe school” programs: community escorts for girls, awareness campaigns against child marriage, adolescent girls’ forums in schools. Provide menstrual hygiene facilities (as pilot MHM programs show school retention improves).
Digital Access in Learning: Set up solar-powered digital kiosks with education content in remote habitations (e.g. Odisha’s DaSha model). Scale up smartphone teaching apps in vernacular languages.
Adult Learning Hubs: Establish evening learning centers in villages where mothers learn basic literacy and vocational skills while younger kids study, creating an incentive synergy.
Data-Driven Local Planning: Train village education committees to use UDISE data to identify out-of-school children and hold schools accountable. Use Mobile apps to report dropouts to panchayats for quick action.
These interventions combine proven strategies (TaRL, nutrition support) with innovations (digital kiosks, mother-tongue). They should be implemented with special focus on states with low literacy rates (e.g. Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan) and high dropouts.
